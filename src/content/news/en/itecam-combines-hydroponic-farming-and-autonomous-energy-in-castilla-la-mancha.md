The Castilla-La Mancha Industrial Technology Center, ITECAM, presented a demonstrator with Parcitank to study how food could be produced autonomously in isolated locations or demanding conditions. Announced ahead of meetechSpain 2026, the proposal combines vertical hydroponic farming with an energy architecture made up of solar panels, batteries, and hydrogen production through electrolysis. Although the reference scenario includes lunar or Martian bases, the technical usefulness also applies to terrestrial problems: energy emergencies, remote facilities, and systems that need to maintain critical services with limited dependence on the grid.

Vertical farming requires control of climate, lighting, and water variables. Parcitank contributes experience in managing the cultivation environment, while the demonstrator adds an energy layer capable of distributing renewable generation among three destinations: direct consumption, battery storage, and electrolysis to produce hydrogen. This separation makes it possible to represent different decisions depending on solar availability, immediate demand, and the need to preserve energy for later periods.

ITECAM says the system can simulate several operating scenarios: a lunar or Martian base, terrestrial conditions, and energy-emergency situations. Simulation does not automatically turn a prototype into a deployment-ready solution, but it makes subsystem dependencies visible. A crop can be productive while remaining vulnerable to a power cut; a battery may cover a short peak, while hydrogen can provide a longer-duration reserve with additional losses and costs. Making these transitions visible is a useful way to evaluate the architecture before building larger facilities.

The story matters because it frames the problem as systems integration, not as a single miracle technology. Vertical farming alone does not guarantee autonomy, and adding renewables does not solve storage management or operational continuity. The demonstrator’s value lies in connecting food production, energy, water, environmental control, and operating scenarios in one interactive model. That logic can apply to rural infrastructure, industrial facilities, and resilience projects even when there is no connection to space exploration.

The project also illustrates the role regional technology centers can play in transferring applied research between companies and research organizations. ITECAM and Parcitank took part in an event bringing together technology centers, companies, and research bodies, and the system was designed to visualize resource allocation in real time. The next technical question is to quantify efficiency, consumption, battery degradation, hydrogen output, and maintenance requirements. Without those metrics, the demonstrator remains a conceptual validation tool; with them, it can become a basis for comparing configurations and deciding where autonomous systems make practical sense.
